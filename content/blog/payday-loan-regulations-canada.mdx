**Payday loan regulations Canada** relies on changed more on one day — January 1, 2025 — than they had in the previous decade, and most articles still quote the old numbers. Here is the short version: the federal government now caps the total cost of a payday loan at **$14 per $100 borrowed** in every province that licenses payday lending, and it lowered the criminal rate of interest from roughly 48% APR to **35% APR**. Provinces still set your maximum loan size, your cooling-off window and your right to an extended payment plan. Two layers, one rulebook — and one significant gap that neither layer covers.

## The two-layer system behind payday loan regulations Canada uses

Almost every "payday loan laws" article treats this as a provincial subject. That was true until 2025. It isn't anymore.

**Layer one is federal.** Section 347 of the Criminal Code makes it an offence to charge above the criminal rate of interest. Section 347.1 carves out payday loans made in a *designated* province — one with its own licensed payday-lending regime — so those loans escape the general ceiling. What changed is that Parliament attached a price to that carve-out. The Criminal Interest Rate Regulations (SOR/2024-114), in force January 1, 2025, set the maximum total cost of borrowing under a payday loan agreement at **14% of the amount advanced**.

**Layer two is provincial.** Each province still runs licensing, sets the maximum loan amount, defines the maximum term, and writes the consumer-protection mechanics: cooling-off periods, rollover bans, extended payment plans, disclosure rules. This is the half of payday loan regulations Canada borrowers usually encounter in person, because it governs what happens at the counter.

The practical effect is simple and widely misreported. Nine provinces have licensed regimes — BC, Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Ontario, New Brunswick, Nova Scotia, PEI and Newfoundland and Labrador. All of them had cost caps at or above $14 per $100 before 2025. The federal number is now the binding one. **Where a provincial cap is higher than the federal cap, the federal cap wins.**

## The 7 rules in payday loan regulations Canada that protect you

These are the rules worth knowing before you sign anything. The first two are federal; the rest are provincial and near-universal across the licensed provinces, with Ontario's version cited where the wording differs. Together they form the working core of payday loan regulations Canada enforces today.

### 1. Your total cost cannot exceed $14 per $100

This is the headline of the current payday loan regulations Canada applies. Borrow $300 and the maximum you can be charged to repay it on your due date is $42, for a total of $342. Borrow $500 and the cap is $70.

Critically, "$14 per $100" is a *total cost of borrowing* limit, not an interest rate. It sweeps in administration fees, brokerage fees, cheque-cashing charges and any other mandatory add-on. A lender cannot charge $14 per $100 and then bolt on a $20 "processing fee."

### 2. The criminal rate is now 35% APR

Outside a licensed payday regime, no lender may exceed **35% APR**. That ceiling replaced the old 60% effective annual rate — about 48% APR — on January 1, 2025.

This matters more than it sounds. If you live in a territory or in Quebec, where there is no designated payday regime, the 35% ceiling applies directly to short-term consumer credit. It is also why you now see so many instalment lenders advertising rates that stop precisely at 34.99%.

### 3. Rollovers are prohibited

A lender cannot let you pay a fee to extend the loan, and cannot issue you a second loan to pay off the first. This rule is the single biggest structural protection in Canadian payday law, because rollovers are what turned a two-week loan into a year-long debt in earlier eras of the industry.

### 4. You get a cooling-off period

In Ontario you can cancel a payday loan within **two business days**, no reason required, and pay back only the principal. Most provinces run the same two-day window. If you signed at 4 p.m. Friday and regretted it Monday morning, you are almost certainly still inside it.

### 5. Repeat borrowing triggers an extended payment plan

Ontario requires that if you take a third payday loan within **63 days**, the lender must offer an extended payment plan letting you repay in instalments rather than one lump sum. Other provinces have comparable repeat-borrowing provisions. Lenders rarely volunteer this — you often have to ask.

### 6. Overdue charges are capped separately

Two costs sit *outside* the $14 cap by design: a dishonoured-cheque or returned-payment fee of up to **$20**, and interest of up to **2.5% per month** on amounts still outstanding after the due date. That monthly figure works out to 30% a year. It is the ceiling, not a suggestion — and it only starts once you are late.

### 7. The lender must be licensed, and disclose in dollars

Every payday lender must hold a provincial licence, display it, and state the cost of your loan **in dollars**, not only as a percentage. This is the enforcement backbone of payday loan regulations Canada relies on — without a licence, none of the other six rules have anyone to answer to. If a website will not tell you which province licenses it, that is not a paperwork oversight. ## Cost caps by province: what changed on January 1, 2025

The table below is the fastest way to see why so much online content about payday loan regulations Canada applies is out of date. The middle column is what the province allowed before the federal regulations; the right column is what a lender can legally charge you today.

| Province | Cap before 2025 | Cap now (federal) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Manitoba | $17 per $100 | **$14 per $100** |
| Saskatchewan | $17 per $100 | **$14 per $100** |
| Ontario | $15 per $100 | **$14 per $100** |
| Alberta | $15 per $100 | **$14 per $100** |
| British Columbia | $15 per $100 | **$14 per $100** |
| Nova Scotia | $15 per $100 | **$14 per $100** |
| New Brunswick | $15 per $100 | **$14 per $100** |
| Prince Edward Island | $15 per $100 | **$14 per $100** |
| Newfoundland and Labrador | $14 per $100 | **$14 per $100** |
| Quebec | No payday regime | 35% APR on all consumer credit |
| Territories (YT, NT, NU) | No payday regime | 35% APR under the Criminal Code |

Two things fall out of this. First, if a lender quotes you $15 or $17 per $100 in 2026, they are quoting a superseded number — stop and check the licence. Second, the borrower who gained most was in Manitoba or Saskatchewan, where the cost of a $500 loan dropped from $85 to $70 overnight.

Even at $14, the annualised cost of a 14-day payday loan is roughly **365% APR**. The cap made payday loans cheaper. It did not make them cheap.

## The one big trap the rules don't cover

Here is the gap. Payday loan regulations Canada enforces apply to *payday loans* — a defined product with a licence attached. They do not reach two products millions of Canadians now use instead.

**Cash advance apps.** Because these apps charge no interest on the advance, they generally sit outside payday-lending legislation entirely. Their revenue comes from optional "tips," express-transfer fees and monthly memberships — none of which are regulated as a cost of borrowing. A $5 express fee on a $100 advance repaid in 14 days is an effective cost well above a triple-digit APR, and no cap applies to it. We break the arithmetic down in our guide to [cash advance apps in Canada](/blog/cash-advance-apps-canada).

**High-cost instalment loans.** The 35% APR ceiling is real, but a $2,000 loan at 34.99% over three years is still expensive credit, and it is entirely legal. Longer terms also mean more total interest, even at a lower rate — run the numbers in our [loan payment calculator](/resources/tools/loan-calculator) before you assume a lower APR means a smaller bill.

The lesson isn't that the rules failed. It's that the rules define a *category*, and the market moved to categories the rules don't name. Compare the total dollars you repay, not the label on the product.

## Who is actually borrowing — and why it matters

The people payday loan regulations Canada was written for are not a hypothetical group, and the federal regulatory analysis put the picture plainly. Canada had **over 600,000 payday borrowers in 2021**, and about 4.52% of Canadians had used a payday loan at some point. **53% of payday borrowers earned less than $55,000 a year**, and 62% said their savings would cover less than three months of expenses. Indigenous respondents used payday loans at roughly 5%, against 1.3% for non-Indigenous respondents.

The FCAC's own borrower survey adds the part that regulation struggles to fix: **half of users took out more than one payday loan** during the survey window, and roughly 30% reported difficulty repaying on time.

That is the honest context for every rule above. The cap lowers the price of a single loan. It does not change what happens when the same shortfall arrives next month.

## What to do if a lender breaks the rules

Enforcement of payday loan regulations Canada operates is provincial, so your complaint goes to the body that issued the licence. If something looks wrong, act in this order:

1. **Check the licence.** Search your provincial consumer protection registry for the lender's name. Unlicensed operators are the source of most complaints.
2. **Recalculate the cost.** Total everything you were charged, divide by the amount advanced. Above 14%? That is a breach.
3. **Use the cooling-off period** if you are still inside it — two business days in most provinces, principal only.
4. **Ask for the extended payment plan** in writing if this is your third loan in about two months.
5. **File a complaint** with the provincial regulator, with your agreement, transfer records and messages attached. Provinces can order refunds and pull licences.
6. **Don't disappear.** Missing payments is the most expensive response available. Our guide to [what happens if you don't pay a payday loan](/blog/what-happens-if-you-dont-pay-a-payday-loan-canada) sets out the real consequences and the better exits.

> **A red flag no regulation can fix:** any lender who asks for a fee *before* releasing your money. Legitimate Canadian lenders deduct their cost from the loan or collect it at repayment. An upfront "insurance," "activation" or "release" payment is the signature of an advance-fee scam, not a compliance quirk.

## The bottom line

The payday loan regulations Canada operates under in 2026 are genuinely stronger than they were two years ago: a hard $14 per $100 ceiling, a 35% APR criminal rate, no rollovers, a two-day cancellation right and a mandatory payment plan for repeat borrowers. Know those five numbers and you can spot a non-compliant offer in under a minute.

What the rules can't do is make a 14-day loan a good deal — $14 per $100 still annualises near 365%. If the same gap keeps reappearing, the answer is a different product, not a cheaper payday loan.
