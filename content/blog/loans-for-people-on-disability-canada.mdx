**Loans for people on disability Canada** lenders approve are usually written about as one category. They are not one category. Five distinct income programs sit behind that phrase, they pay wildly different amounts, they arrive on different schedules, and one of them has a scheduled reduction already written into law. A lender assessing you will not distinguish between them. You should.

## Quick Answer

Disability income qualifies for borrowing everywhere in Canada — provincial caps are set against *net income*, not wages. What differs is the amount, the payment date, and the stability. CPP disability pays up to $1,741.20 a month; provincial programs range from roughly $1,436 to $1,940; the federal Canada Disability Benefit adds up to $204.20. In Alberta, a large group of recipients has a **$200 monthly reduction scheduled for 1 January 2028** — which matters enormously if you sign a multi-year loan today.

| Program | 2026 maximum, single | Paid |
|---|---|---|
| CPP Disability (federal, contributory) | $1,741.20/mo | Monthly, late in month |
| Alberta AISH | $1,940/mo | Monthly |
| Alberta ADAP (new, from 2 July 2026) | $1,740/mo + $200 transition | Monthly |
| BC Disability Assistance (PWD) | $1,483.50/mo | Monthly |
| Ontario ODSP | $1,436/mo | Monthly |
| Canada Disability Benefit (federal, tops up) | $204.20/mo | Monthly |

Two structural points follow immediately.

First, **every one of these is monthly**. Payday loans are built around biweekly pay cycles. If a repayment is scheduled to a "next payday" default, it will eventually land in the emptiest part of your month. Ask for the debit date to sit two or three days after your deposit, and get it written into the agreement.

Second, **the Canada Disability Benefit stacks**. It is a federal top-up for people aged 18 to 64 who are approved for the Disability Tax Credit, worth up to $204.20 monthly for the July 2026 to June 2027 period, with a one-time $150 supplement being paid from September 2026. Full amounts go to those with adjusted family net income under $23,000, phasing to zero at $35,000 — or $45,000 where at least $10,000 comes from work. If you qualify for the DTC and have not applied, that is the highest-return hour available to you, and it does not need repaying.

## The Alberta Cliff Nobody Is Pricing In

This is the fact worth the whole article. From **2 July 2026** Alberta operates the Alberta Disability Assistance Program alongside AISH. Recipients who remain on AISH keep the $1,940 maximum. Recipients moved to ADAP receive **$1,740 — two hundred dollars less — plus a $200 monthly transition amount that is scheduled to end on 31 December 2027.**

Read that as a lender would. Today an ADAP recipient's bank statements show $1,940 a month. On 1 January 2028 they show $1,740. The transition amount is real money and it is genuinely paid, but it has an expiry date, and no automated income check reads expiry dates.

If you are in Alberta and considering an instalment loan that runs past 2027, the underwriting assumption behind your approval expires before your loan does. That is not a reason to avoid borrowing. It is a reason to size the payment against $1,740, not $1,940, and to prefer a term that ends before the step-down rather than one that straddles it.

## Loans for People on Disability Canada and the Legal Shield Gap

Disability income is among the most protected money in Canadian law.

Section 65(1) of the *Canada Pension Plan* provides that a benefit *"shall not be assigned, charged, attached, anticipated or given as security, and any transaction purporting to assign, charge, attach, anticipate or give as security a benefit is void."* Section 65(1.1) adds that a benefit *"is exempt from seizure and execution, either at law or in equity."* Provincially, section 18(1) of Ontario's *ODSP Act* protects income support from garnishment, attachment, execution, seizure or receivership, and Ontario's own guidance confirms that social assistance and pension payments cannot be garnished even after they land in a bank account.

So a lender who sued you and won could not reach this income. A clause in a loan agreement purporting to take your CPP as security is void by statute — not unenforceable in practice, but void.

Then there is the pre-authorized debit, which is none of those things. It is not an assignment, a charge, a seizure or an execution. It is you instructing your own bank to pay. The entire statutory shield remains intact while the money leaves anyway.

That is the honest framing of disability borrowing: the law protects this income from every route except the one you open yourself.

## How Lenders Actually Read a Disability Income File

Automated assessment does not know what your income *is*. It knows what your account shows, and that gap creates three predictable problems on disability applications.

**It reads amounts, not sources.** A $1,940 monthly deposit and a $1,940 salary look identical. That is broadly good news — it is why approval rates on benefit income are high — but it means nothing in the system flags that this income is fixed, indexed, or scheduled to change.

**It reads the last 60 to 90 days.** A one-off supplement inside that window inflates the apparent average. The Canada Disability Benefit's $150 lump sum being paid from September 2026 will sit in exactly that window for applications made in the autumn, and an automated check will treat it as ordinary income.

**It reads the pre-deposit low point.** The number that decides most applications is what your balance does in the days *before* money arrives, not the monthly average. On a single monthly deposit that trough is deeper and longer than on a biweekly cycle, which is why identical incomes are assessed differently depending on how often they land.

The practical response is to do the reading yourself before applying. Take your last three statements, find the lowest balance in each month, and size any repayment against that figure rather than against the deposit. If the payment does not clear comfortably at the trough, it will fail eventually — regardless of what the approval says.

## Loans for People on Disability Canada: Five Steps to Borrow Safely

1. **Claim everything first.** The DTC and the Canada Disability Benefit, provincial mandatory and discretionary benefits, drug and dental coverage, and any assistive-device program. Money you do not repay beats money you do.
2. **Check how your program treats loans.** Some provincial programs treat borrowed money as income unless it is for an approved purpose. Ontario's ODSP is the strictest example — a loan outside the approved list can be deducted from your support. Ask your caseworker *before* borrowing, not after.
3. **Size the payment against your floor, not your ceiling.** Use the lowest monthly income you can foresee over the loan term. In Alberta that means $1,740. Everywhere it means excluding one-time supplements.
4. **Move the debit date.** Two to three days after your deposit lands, in writing, before you sign.
5. **Prefer products that report.** Payday loans generally build no credit history when repaid on time, but reach the bureaus through collections if they fail. A credit-union instalment loan or a credit-builder product costs less and leaves you better off.

## The Bottom Line

Lenders assessing loans for people on disability Canada wide treat this as ordinary income, which is correct and, on balance, good. But they treat it as *interchangeable* income, which it is not. It arrives monthly rather than biweekly, it is protected from creditors in a way wages are not, and in one province it is scheduled to fall.

Size the loan to the income you will have in 2028, not the income on this month's statement.
