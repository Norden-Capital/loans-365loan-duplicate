The **Canada trade surplus** widened to **$3.9 billion in June 2026**, up from $3.7 billion in May, according to figures Statistics Canada released on August 4, 2026. Merchandise exports rose 0.4% to a record **$77.5 billion** — a fifth consecutive monthly increase — while imports edged up 0.2% to a record high of **$73.6 billion**. It was the fourth consecutive month in surplus. But the same release contains a detail that complicates the headline: the Canadian dollar posted its largest monthly decline in nearly four years, and measured in US dollars, trade in both directions actually fell.

## What the Canada Trade Surplus Report Actually Shows

Five figures define the release.

1. **The balance widened.** At $3.9 billion, June marked a fourth consecutive monthly surplus — a run RBC Economics noted in its same-day analysis.
2. **Exports set a record.** Statistics Canada described $77.5 billion as "a record," and it capped a five-month climb during which exports rose **22.8%**.
3. **Imports also set a record.** At $73.6 billion, imports reached what the agency called "a record high," with imports from the United States also setting a record in June.
4. **Composition shifted sharply.** Metal and non-metallic mineral products rose 16.5%, led by unwrought gold at +27.9%, and copper ore exports hit a record $934 million. That strength offset a 10.0% fall in energy exports.
5. **The currency moved more than the volumes.** The Canadian dollar fell 1.7 cents US against May — the largest monthly decrease since October 2022.

Those five figures are the whole of the Canada trade surplus story on the goods side. Services trade told a quieter story. Service exports edged down 0.2% to $20.8 billion and service imports fell 0.4% to $21.0 billion, leaving a small services deficit that trims the combined picture.

## Background: How the Canada Trade Surplus Built Over Five Months

June's report is not an isolated month. Exports have now risen for five consecutive months, gaining 22.8% across that stretch, and the trade balance has been positive since March. That is a marked change from 2025, when tariff disruption and weak business investment weighed on the trade account.

Context matters for interpreting the strength, though. A large share of the recent export gain is concentrated in gold and other metals rather than in manufactured goods — a pattern that reflects commodity prices and investor demand as much as it reflects Canadian industrial capacity. Energy, historically the country's largest export category, moved the other way in June: crude oil exports fell 11.1% and refined petroleum products dropped 16.9%, both driven by lower prices rather than lower volumes.

## The Catch: A Weaker Loonie Flatters the Headline

This is the part of the release most worth understanding, and it is why the Canada trade surplus should be read with care.

Statistics Canada publishes trade in Canadian dollars. When the loonie weakens, the same physical shipment converts into more Canadian dollars — so both exports and imports can look larger without anything changing in the real economy. In June, the currency fell enough to flip the sign: exports rose 0.4% in Canadian dollars but **fell 2.0% in US dollars**, and imports rose 0.2% in Canadian dollars but **fell 2.1% in US dollars**.

The Globe and Mail led its coverage on exactly this point, describing the surplus as growing "as weak loonie boosts value of imports, exports." Neither measure is wrong. But a household reading "record exports" should know that a meaningful part of the record is an exchange-rate effect.

## Why This Matters for Canadian Households

Trade statistics feel remote from a monthly budget, and a Canada trade surplus is not something anyone banks. Three channels connect them anyway.

**Import prices.** A weaker dollar raises the landed cost of anything Canada buys abroad — food, electronics, vehicles, parts. Those increases typically reach shelves with a lag of weeks to months, which means the June currency move is a forward-looking signal about prices rather than a description of today's.

**Jobs in trade-exposed sectors.** Five straight months of export growth is genuinely supportive of employment in mining, metals and related logistics. It is less reassuring for energy-region employment, where June's 10.0% export decline was price-driven and margins compress quickly when crude falls.

**Interest-rate expectations.** A softer currency tends to push imported-goods inflation up, which complicates the case for rate cuts. For anyone carrying variable-rate debt or planning to borrow, that argues for deciding on today's rates rather than on an assumed cut. ## Key Takeaways

- The Canada trade surplus reached **$3.9 billion** in June 2026, a fourth consecutive monthly surplus.
- Exports hit a **record $77.5 billion**, up 22.8% over five months; imports hit a **record $73.6 billion**.
- Measured in **US dollars, exports fell 2.0% and imports fell 2.1%** — the currency did much of the work.
- The loonie's **1.7-cent drop** was its largest monthly decline since October 2022.
- Gold and metals carried the export gain while **energy exports fell 10.0%** on lower prices.

## What to Watch Next

Statistics Canada releases the **July Labour Force Survey on August 7, 2026** and **June building permits on August 12**. The jobs report is the more consequential of the two for households: it will indicate whether the export run is translating into hiring, or whether the strength remains concentrated in commodity values that create relatively few jobs.

## The Bottom Line

The June Canada trade surplus produced a genuinely strong report — a widening surplus, two records, and a fifth straight month of export growth. It also produced a caution. Strip out the exchange-rate effect and the underlying volumes softened, while the currency move behind the headline is the same one that will make imported goods more expensive later this year. For households, the practical response is unchanged: keep some slack in the budget, and treat a strong trade number as context rather than as a reason to take on more fixed commitments.
