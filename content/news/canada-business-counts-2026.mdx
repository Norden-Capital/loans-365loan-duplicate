The **Canada business counts 2026** release published by Statistics Canada on 14 August shows **1.37 million employer businesses** and **3.78 million non-employer businesses** with annual revenues above $30,000. Set against the same release a year earlier, the two lines are moving in opposite directions — and the gap between them says something useful about how Canadians are actually earning a living.

![Canada business counts 2026 — a small business owner reviewing records and paperwork at a desk](https://images.pexels.com/photos/8062357/pexels-photo-8062357.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1200)

## Canada Business Counts 2026: Summary of the New Data

- **1.37 million employer businesses** in June 2026, down from 1.38 million a year earlier
- **3.78 million non-employer businesses**, up from 3.59 million — an increase of roughly **190,000**
- Non-employer businesses now outnumber employers by about **2.8 to one**
- Only businesses with **annual revenues above $30,000** are counted
- Figures come from the **Business Register**, compiled mainly from Canada Revenue Agency tax records

## The Headline Numbers

Statistics Canada produces these counts twice a year, for June and December, publishing in August and February. They are a point-in-time snapshot of active businesses rather than a survey of people, which makes them a different kind of measure from the monthly jobs numbers.

| Measure | June 2025 | June 2026 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Employer businesses | 1.38 million | **1.37 million** | Down slightly |
| Non-employer businesses (revenue > $30,000) | 3.59 million | **3.78 million** | **Up ~190,000** |
| Ratio, non-employer to employer | ~2.6 : 1 | **~2.8 : 1** | Widening |

An important caveat belongs up front. Statistics Canada explicitly cautions that period-to-period changes in these counts warrant careful interpretation, because they can reflect administrative or methodological adjustments to the Business Register rather than businesses opening and closing. The direction is worth noting; the precision is not worth over-reading.

## Why the Canada Business Counts 2026 Figures Diverge From the Jobs Data

Here is the part most coverage will miss. The business counts and the Labour Force Survey are measuring different things, and this year the gap between them is unusually informative.

The **Labour Force Survey** counts *people*, classified by their main job. Its July 2026 release recorded self-employed workers up 44,000 (+1.6%) on the month, and up 73,000 (+2.7%) since April.

The **Business Register** counts *businesses* that file with the CRA and clear the $30,000 revenue threshold. It recorded roughly 190,000 more of them than a year earlier.

Those two numbers are not contradictory, but they are not the same size either. A person whose main job is a salaried position, who also runs a business earning $45,000 on the side, appears once in the Business Register and not at all in the self-employment line of the Labour Force Survey.

The most reasonable reading is that a meaningful share of the growth in registered businesses sits **alongside** employment rather than replacing it. That is consistent with the survey data rather than in conflict with it — and it is a different economic story from the one usually told about self-employment.

## Background: What the Canada Business Counts 2026 Register Measures

The Business Register is built primarily from Canada Revenue Agency records, which gives it strengths and blind spots worth understanding before drawing conclusions.

**What it captures well:** every entity that files, across all provinces, territories, census metropolitan areas and census subdivisions. It is comprehensive rather than sampled.

**What it leaves out:** any business earning $30,000 a year or less. A great many side businesses, first-year ventures and part-time operations fall under that line and never appear in these counts at all. The 3.78 million figure is therefore a floor, not a total.

**What it cannot tell you:** whether the owner is thriving. A registered business with $35,000 of revenue and one with $3.5 million both count as one.

For context on the wider economy in the same release week, Statistics Canada also reported manufacturing sales of $78.8 billion in June, up 0.1% on the month and 9.3% for the second quarter, and wholesale sales of $92.5 billion, up 2.8% on the month and 9.0% year over year.

## Why This Matters for Working Canadians

The practical consequence of the shift in the Canada business counts 2026 figures lands on anyone whose income does not arrive as a single predictable salary.

1. **Income evidence gets harder.** An employer business generates T4s and pay stubs. A non-employer business generates invoices, irregular deposits and a tax return that arrives once a year, months out of date.
2. **The safety net is thinner.** Self-employed Canadians do not pay EI premiums on business income by default and are not covered by regular EI benefits unless they opt into the special benefits programme.
3. **Cash flow replaces payday.** Money arrives when clients pay, not on the 15th and the 30th. That single fact reshapes budgeting, borrowing and every automatic payment attached to an account.
4. **Assessment has quietly adapted.** Most lenders now read 60 to 90 days of bank transaction data rather than employment documents, which reads business income far better than a pay stub ever did.

## Canada Business Counts 2026: Key Takeaways

- Employer businesses **edged down** to 1.37 million while non-employer businesses **rose to 3.78 million**
- The roughly **190,000 increase** far exceeds the rise in self-employment recorded by the Labour Force Survey
- The gap is consistent with growth in businesses run **alongside** a job, not only instead of one
- The **$30,000 revenue threshold** means the true number of small ventures is higher than 3.78 million
- Statistics Canada **cautions against over-reading** period-to-period changes in these counts

## Conclusion

The Canada business counts 2026 data will not make many front pages, which is part of why it is worth reading. It records a slow structural change: fewer businesses with payrolls, more Canadians earning through an entity of their own, and a widening share of national income that arrives irregularly rather than on a schedule.

For households, the useful takeaway is not the headline count but what follows from it. Income that does not arrive on a payday needs a budget, and a borrowing approach, built around cash flow rather than around a pay cycle.
